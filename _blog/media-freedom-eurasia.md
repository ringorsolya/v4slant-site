---
title: "Media Freedom, Bias, and Manipulation in the Eurasian Post-Socialist Space"
order: 9
summary: A machine-learning model measures media freedom through sentiment towards influential politicians in over one million articles from five countries.
---
This paper explores media freedom, bias, and manipulation in the Eurasian post-socialist space, proposing a novel machine-learning model to evaluate media freedom through sentiment analysis. The study defines media freedom as the ability of journalists to safely criticise influential politicians, measured by the relative sentiment in media coverage. Using over one million articles from five countries – Russia, Kazakhstan, Belarus, Ukraine, and Poland – published between 2017 and 2020, the paper investigates differences in media environments across democratic and autocratic regimes.

## Disparities in media freedom

The results highlight significant disparities in media freedom. Kazakhstan and Belarus have the most constrained environments, with positive sentiment about influential politicians dominating the state-controlled media. In Belarus, independent or niche outlets occasionally publish critical views, but their reach is limited compared to mass-circulation newspapers. Kazakhstan's media is highly supportive of influential elites, reflecting significant government control. In contrast, Poland emerges as the most media-free environment among the studied countries. Despite increasing polarisation and government influence in some outlets, Polish media retains the ability to critically assess political leaders, reflecting robust democratic norms and institutional safeguards. Ukraine's media landscape is transitional, with characteristics of both free democracies and more controlled systems. Some outlets owned by oligarchs exhibit a mix of positive and critical coverage, depending on political alignments.

Russia presents a complex case. While online media appears more balanced than state-controlled television, evidence of manipulation distorts the perception of freedom. For example, mentions of influential politicians decline during politically or economically unfavourable periods. This suggests sophisticated media control strategies that blend censorship with narrative management. The findings align with the notion of "spin autocrats" who manipulate public opinion through subtle methods rather than outright repression.

## Elections

The study also examines media behaviour during presidential elections, revealing sharp contrasts between democratic and autocratic contexts. In democracies like Poland and Ukraine, mentions of presidential candidates rise significantly during election periods, often accompanied by a decline in sentiment scores, reflecting increased scrutiny by the press. Conversely, autocratic regimes like Russia, Belarus, and Kazakhstan tightly control election narratives, portraying ruling elites positively while limiting exposure to opposition candidates. For example, Belarusian media heavily praised President Lukashenko before the 2020 elections, but the sentiment shifted as public dissent grew following the contested results.

## Detecting censorship and manipulation

To detect censorship and manipulation, the paper uses economic indicators such as currency fluctuations to analyse changes in media sentiment. Findings reveal minimal manipulation in most countries except Russia, where influential politicians are mentioned less frequently during periods of economic downturn, suggesting a deliberate strategy to suppress unfavourable narratives. Despite this, Russia's online media still exhibits some level of freedom compared to other forms of mass media.

The paper introduces a scalable, data-driven approach to assessing media freedom using machine learning. By incorporating sentiment and topic modelling, the model reduces subjectivity and enables nuanced analyses of media environments across diverse contexts. The findings underscore the importance of free media in supporting democratic accountability and highlight the ongoing challenges of censorship and manipulation in post-socialist countries. This approach provides a valuable tool for future comparative studies of media freedom.
